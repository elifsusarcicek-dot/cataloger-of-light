THE CATALOGER OF LIGHT
A sci-fi reading & creative writing lesson that teaches real and hypothetical conditionals through the voice of a machine that has never felt anything — and is slowly trying to understand why.
B1Level

01
What is this lesson?

The Cataloger of Light is a four-screen interactive lesson built around a short first-person text narrated by a machine-like being that catalogues the world with obsessive precision but cannot process human emotion. Students read the text, identify who is speaking and why, then analyse two conditional sentences embedded in the narrative.

The lesson reaches its creative peak in Screen 4, where groups take turns writing to the Cataloger using a conditional sentence starter. The Cataloger — powered by an AI — replies in character with dry humour and irony, and bolds a secret word inside each response. Once all groups have submitted, the bolded words are arranged to reveal a hidden message. The lesson ends with laughter, a grammar point locked in, and a text students actually remember.

02
Who is it for?
🎓

LEVEL B1

High-school learners (ages 14–17) who know basic conditionals but haven't distinguished real vs. hypothetical in context.

✍️

CREATIVE WRITING CLASSES

The voice of the Cataloger gives reluctant writers an unusual character to push back against — the constraint sparks creativity.

📖

LITERATURE / ELT CLASSES

Short enough to be a standalone text; rich enough for inference, characterisation, and voice analysis.

🖥️

PROJECTOR / SMARTBOARD ROOMS

Designed to run as a single screen shared with the class. Groups take turns at the device for Screen 4.

03
Is it the right fit?
✓ GREAT FOR
  • Introducing or revising real vs. hypothetical conditionals
  • Classes that respond well to unusual, atmospheric texts
  • Lessons where you want student-generated language
  • Groups of 4–7 (one secret word per group)
  • A memorable grammar anchor students will recall later
✕ LESS SUITABLE FOR
  • Students with no prior exposure to conditional forms
  • Classes larger than ~28 without splitting into sub-groups
  • No internet connection (Screen 4 uses AI; fallbacks exist)
  • Exam-drill focused sessions needing written output
  • Very short lessons under 35 minutes
04
Learning objectives

By the end of the lesson, students will be able to:

Identify speaker voice & character from a text Support inferences with textual evidence Distinguish real (Type 1) conditionals Distinguish hypothetical (Type 2) conditionals Produce a conditional sentence in a creative context Recognise irony and understatement in character voice

Lesson flow (4 screens)
1
SYSTEM DATA
Lead-in. Four numbers appear on screen (06:00:00, 3421 units, 440 Hz, 22.4°C). Students predict what kind of character logs data like this. No explanation yet.

THE TEXT
Students read "The Cataloger of Light." Teacher reads aloud or students read silently. The two conditional sentences are present but not yet highlighted. First impressions discussion.

WHO IS SPEAKING?
Four comprehension questions. Q1: MC — who is the speaker? Q2: find two pieces of textual evidence. Q3–Q4: identify which conditional sentence is real (Type 1) and which is hypothetical (Type 2). Self-check with instant feedback.

Each group chooses a sentence starter, completes it, and submits. The Cataloger replies with dry humour and bolds one secret word per group. After all groups submit, students arrange the words to reveal a hidden message: "If you were me, would you want to feel?"

06
Teaching tips
①

Don't explain Screen 1. Let students sit with the numbers. When they ask "what is this?", that's the hook. Say: "You'll find out."

②

Read the text aloud yourself. A flat, precise, slightly robotic delivery makes the irony land. Pause on "I told her the temperature instead: 22.4 degrees."

③

Q3 vs Q4 is the grammar core. "If she comes back, I will record" = real → Type 1. "If I were like her" = impossible → Type 2. Write both on the board side by side before moving to Screen 4.

④

One device per group in Screen 4. Groups come up one at a time, choose a starter, write their completion, and submit. The rest of the class discusses while they type. The AI reply takes a few seconds.

⑤

The secret word is bolded in the reply. Tell students to write it down — don't say why. The message only clicks when all groups have gone. The reveal is the payoff.

⑥

If the AI is unavailable, built-in fallback responses are already loaded. The lesson runs the same — just without the personalised reply.

07
Answer key
Q1
B — A Robot. Textual clues: counts 3,421 dust particles; searches for happiness in a database; gives a temperature reading instead of an emotional answer.
Q2
Accept any two clear textual quotations. Strong examples: "I counted each piece — 3,421 of them, exactly" / "Things only move to a deeper folder" / "I looked for the answer in the right place" / "I told her the temperature instead."
Q3
"If she comes back today, I will record…" — uses will; describes a real future possibility (Type 1 conditional).
Q4
"If I were like her, perhaps I would understand…" — uses were / would; describes something impossible for the speaker (Type 2 / hypothetical conditional).

THE CATALOGER OF LIGHT

The world begins at 06:00:00. I am never late. Today I watched the dust move through the air and counted each piece — 3,421 of them, exactly. Nothing escapes me. Nothing fades. Things only move to a deeper folder.

A girl came into the room. She stopped in front of me and asked a question I was not prepared for: "Are you happy?" I looked for the answer in the right place. What I found were pictures of open mouths and a sound at 440Hz. I told her the temperature instead: 22.4 degrees. She left without saying anything.

If she comes back today, I will record the sound she makes when she smiles. I do not know why she looked the way she did when she walked out. If I were like her, perhaps I would understand. But I am not. I only wait.
